## Slide 4: Key terms in plain words (1/2)
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 33 seconds, 82 words)

Here are the key terms you will hear all day. The H-S, or Harmonized System, is a worldwide product code with six digits. The H-T-S is the American tariff schedule, which adds US-specific digits and the duty rates. It is published by the U-S-I-T-C. C-B-P, U.S. Customs and Border Protection, applies and enforces the rules. A-C-E is the electronic system for entries. And the importer of record is the party legally responsible for the import, even when a broker files for them.


## Slide 6: EU versus US at a glance
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 35 seconds, 88 words)

If you know the E-U system, you already know most of what you need. The classification method, the General Rules of Interpretation, is identical. What differs is the legal text, which in the US is the H-T-S-U-S with ten digits, how binding decisions work, and who is responsible. In the US it is always the importer of record. Extra duties are not just a measure in the tariff, they sit in a special chapter, ninety-nine. And records must be kept for five years from the date of entry.


## Slide 8: Classification in the import flow
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 30 seconds, 75 words)

Classification is not a one-time event. It starts with product data, such as function, material and use. We then decide the ten-digit code. The broker uses it to file the entry in A-C-E. The importer pays duty. Months later, C-B-P liquidates the entry, which means it finalizes the duty. If something was wrong, there are ways to correct it after entry. Because the code sits in master data, one wrong decision repeats on every shipment.


## Slide 10: What is the Harmonized System?
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 33 seconds, 82 words)

The Harmonized System is the world's common language for products. It was created by the World Customs Organization and has been in force since nineteen eighty-eight. More than one hundred fifty countries use it. Think of it like a library catalogue: every product has exactly one shelf number. The first six digits are identical everywhere. After that, each country adds its own digits. A new edition, H-S twenty twenty-seven, starts in January twenty twenty-seven, so we need to review our master data.


## Slide 12: US legal framework
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 26 seconds, 66 words)

In the United States, one body writes the tariff and another applies it. The U-S-I-T-C publishes the H-T-S, based on law and presidential decisions. C-B-P applies it at the border and enforces it. The Census Bureau handles statistics and the Department of Commerce handles antidumping cases. Because the tariff changes often, always write down which revision you used. Today that is Revision twenty-three of twenty twenty-six.


## Slide 14: Organization of the HTSUS
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 33 seconds, 83 words)

The American tariff has several parts. At the start you find the General Notes and the General Rules of Interpretation. Then come twenty-two sections and chapters one to ninety-seven, which contain the product descriptions, notes and base duty rates. At the end are two special chapters. Chapter ninety-eight gives special treatment such as duty relief for returned goods. Chapter ninety-nine adds or changes duties through trade measures. Beginners often search only the product chapters and miss the notes and the last two chapters.

## Slide 20: Keyword search: a finding aid

The keyword search is useful, but it is only a finding aid. If we search for dental implant, we get twenty-three results, many of them irrelevant. Use it to find candidate headings, then browse the chapter and read the notes. The legal answer never comes from a keyword hit alone. ## Slide 24: GRI 1 and 2 as published
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 28 seconds, 71 words)

This is the original legal text of the first two rules. Rule one says classification follows the terms of the headings and the relevant notes, and that titles and the index are only for reference. Rule two says that an unfinished or unassembled article is treated as the finished article, if it has the essential character. And it says that a heading for a material also covers mixtures of that material.


## Slide 26: GRI 3 as published
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 24 seconds, 60 words)

Rule three is the tie-breaker, used only when two or more headings could fit. Step a: choose the most specific description. Step b: if that does not work, for mixtures, composite goods or sets for retail sale, use the component that gives the essential character. Step c: as a last resort, choose the heading that comes last in numerical order.


## Slide 28: Additional U.S. Rules (ARI)
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 36 seconds, 89 words)

The United States adds four extra rules on top of the international ones. Rule one a says that if the tariff depends on use, you look at the principal use in the US of goods of that kind. Rule one b covers actual use, which needs proof within three years. Rule one c explains parts: a parts provision covers goods solely or principally used as a part, but a general parts heading never beats a specific one. These rules apply only where the tariff text does not say otherwise.


## Slide 30: Classification flow, step by step
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 29 seconds, 73 words)

Here is the full method in seven steps. One: collect the facts, such as function, material, design, packaging and origin. Two: find candidate headings. Three: read the notes. Four: apply the General Rules of Interpretation in order. Five: select the subheading, comparing only equal levels. Six: check chapters ninety-eight and ninety-nine. Seven: document the decision and get it approved. If you are stuck, ask for a ruling or escalate to Global Trade Compliance.


## Slide 32: Example 2: dental handpiece
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 35 seconds, 87 words)

Our second example is a dental handpiece. The key question is whether it counts as a motor-driven dental drill engine, which is heading nine zero one eight point four one, or a hand instrument, which falls under nine zero one eight point four nine. Parts and accessories need extra checks under Note two of Chapter ninety. The facts that matter are: does it include a motor, what is the power source, and is it sold as a unit or separately. Keep the specification sheet in the file.


## Slide 34: Legal weight of notes
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 26 seconds, 66 words)

The tariff has layers of legal text. On top are the General Rules of Interpretation. Below them come section notes, then chapter notes, then subheading and additional US notes. Notes can exclude goods, define terms, or send specific goods to a heading. In practice, the notes are the small print that wins arguments. So the rule is simple: read the notes before you decide between headings.


## Slide 36: Section notes in the HTS tool
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 24 seconds, 59 words)

Next to Chapter Notes you also find Section Notes. A section groups several chapters. Section eighteen covers optical, medical and measuring instruments, clocks and musical instruments. Its notes are the house rules for all those chapters. Always read both tabs. And remember that the chapter download link gives you the complete text including the U-S notes at the end.


## Slide 38: WCO Explanatory Notes: basics
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 31 seconds, 78 words)

The Explanatory Notes are the official user manual of the Harmonized System, published by the World Customs Organization. They explain what each heading covers, what it includes and what it excludes. In the United States they are not legally binding, but customs and courts treat them as persuasive. If the law itself, meaning the headings and the notes, says something different, the law wins. You will find the Explanatory Notes through W-C-O publications, not on the H-T-S website.


## Slide 40: Hierarchy of authorities
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 25 seconds, 62 words)

Not all sources weigh the same. At the top is the law: the tariff text, the notes and the General Rules of Interpretation. Next come court decisions. Then C-B-P rulings, including your own binding rulings. Explanatory Notes and W-C-O opinions are persuasive. At the bottom are broker advice, supplier proposals and keyword hits. They are input, never the basis of a decision.


## Slide 42: CBP binding rulings
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 32 seconds, 81 words)

A binding ruling is a written answer from C-B-P about how specific goods are classified. It is the American counterpart to the Binding Tariff Information in the E-U. It protects the requester for the transaction described, as long as the facts match. Rulings are published, so you can search them. There are N-Y rulings from the commodity specialists and H-Q rulings from headquarters. A request must be specific and include samples or specifications, and you can ask to redact confidential data.


## Slide 44: When to request a binding ruling
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 26 seconds, 66 words)

When should you request a binding ruling? Yes, when volumes or duties are high, when the product is new, when two headings are possible, or when you want to use a special provision in chapter ninety-eight. Maybe, for product families with only small variations. No, when the classification is clear. Plan for lead time of several weeks, and store the ruling number in your master data.


## Slide 46: GRI 2(a): unfinished goods
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 36 seconds, 89 words)

Rule two a deals with goods that are not finished. If an article is incomplete or unfinished but already has the essential character of the finished article, it is classified as the finished article. The same applies if it arrives unassembled or disassembled. Think of a bicycle without a saddle: still a bicycle. But a bar of metal is not yet a bicycle, even if it is meant to become one. Ask what is missing, whether it is minor, and whether the article is already shaped for its use.

## Slide 54: Chapter 98: special provisions
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 26 seconds, 65 words)

Chapter ninety-eight is the discount chapter. It contains special provisions that can reduce or remove duty in specific situations: goods returned to the United States, repairs, assembly abroad, temporary imports under bond, and articles for persons with disabilities under the Nairobi Protocol. But each provision has strict conditions, and you need proof. Never claim one without checking the conditions and agreeing with Global Trade Compliance.


## Slide 56: Ch. 98 example: 9817.00.96
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 23 seconds, 58 words)

This is the Nairobi Protocol provision for articles specially designed for persons with disabilities. The duty is Free, which is attractive. But eligibility is defined in the notes, and customs rulings decide which articles qualify. Do not assume that dental or implant products qualify. Claim it only with documented evidence and with the agreement of Global Trade Compliance.


## Slide 58: Chapter 99: two layers of duty
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 32 seconds, 80 words)

In an entry, duty often has two layers. Line one is the normal ten-digit code from chapters one to ninety-seven, with its base duty, for example Free. Line two, if it applies, is a chapter ninety-nine code that adds a duty or grants an exception. Which line applies depends mainly on the country of origin, meaning where the product was made, not where it was shipped from. Always check whether measures stack, which exclusions apply, and keep proof of origin.


## Slide 60: Ch. 99 example: Section 232
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 30 seconds, 76 words)

Section two thirty-two applies to articles of steel, aluminum and copper and to some derivative articles. Heading nine nine zero three point eight two point zero two adds fifty percent on top of the normal duty. The next heading creates an exception for certain articles where the metal makes up less than fifteen percent of the weight. So for metal instruments, trays or hardware, you need composition data and weight shares from the bill of materials.


## Slide 62: AD/CVD: trade remedies
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 28 seconds, 70 words)

Antidumping and countervailing duties are remedies against unfair trade, imposed after investigations by the Department of Commerce and the I-T-C. C-B-P collects them as cash deposits at entry. The important rule: the written scope of the order decides, not the tariff code. So for each new code or material, check the case lists. For Straumann, this is relevant mostly for components such as fasteners, packaging or tools. Document the check.

## Slide 66: Reasonable care in practice
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 30 seconds, 76 words)

What does reasonable care look like in practice? For each expectation we need a control and evidence. People who classify must be qualified, so we need role descriptions and training records. Facts must be complete, so we need data sheets and specifications. The method must be consistent, so we use a standard memo. We check authorities and keep a log. We monitor changes. And we correct errors. A control without proof is invisible to an auditor.

## Slide 70: Choosing the correction route
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 29 seconds, 72 words)

There are three main routes. A post summary correction fixes entries that are not yet liquidated, within about three hundred days of entry. A protest challenges a decision after liquidation, within one hundred eighty days. And a prior disclosure is used when there may have been a violation, before the authorities start a formal investigation, to reduce penalties. Which route fits depends on the status of the entry and on legal advice.

## Slide 74: Why Chapter 99 raises the stakes
HEYGEN VIDEO SCRIPT (about 30 seconds, 75 words)

Here is an illustration with placeholder numbers. A shipment worth one million dollars has a base duty of zero. If a chapter ninety-nine heading adds ten percent, that is one hundred thousand dollars. If this is not declared, the duty loss is one hundred thousand plus interest. A negligence penalty could reach up to two hundred thousand, and gross negligence up to four hundred thousand. So a free product can still create a six-figure problem.
